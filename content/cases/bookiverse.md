## Background

Reading and writing remain disconnected in a hyper-connected world. New writers struggle to be seen without an existing audience or platform support, and most reading platforms treat readers as passive consumers. At the same time, younger readers expect to take part: to give feedback, follow the process and engage in real time. Publishing has been slow to adopt that kind of interaction, and while the global publishing market grows steadily, the creator economy is growing much faster.

My question: **How might we help emerging authors gain visibility and connect with readers to grow together?**

My hypothesis: if a platform invites active participation rather than simple consumption, it can bridge the gap between authors and readers.

## Discovery

**Interviews.** I ran two semi-structured in-depth interviews over Zoom, about 50 minutes each with a set of 16 questions, recorded and transcribed. One was with an independent author in Korea who publishes on Brunch, a Korean blogging and writing platform. The other was with an office worker who reads heavily and likes to find new writers.

The author described what it feels like to go unread:

- "Even if you work hard, if the views are below 50, you feel helpless."
- "Two genuinely posted comments are much more helpful than 200 likes."
- "If the initial fan acquisition, feedback, and sponsorship take place in one space, I think I'll have the courage to continue my creation."

The reader described the other side of the same gap:

- "It's so hard to spot a rookie writer. The platform doesn't actively support rookies."
- There is no place to talk to an author she likes, and she worries when a new writer's series stops.
- She would enjoy reading early, leaving feedback and supporting a writer, and thought she would "quickly become a fan" that way.

From the two interviews I drew four insights:

1. **Disconnection between new writers and readers.** The discovery gap is the core structural problem.
2. **Relationships drive both creation and consumption.** Writers keep going when they know someone reads them; readers stay when they feel close to the writer.
3. **Current platforms lack a growth system.** Growth data and the discovery experience need to come together.
4. **Strong demand for taking part in the creative process.** A platform built around creation and participation is the opportunity.

**Comparing platforms.** I compared six reading and writing platforms, three global and three Korean.

| Platform | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Wattpad | Massive user base | Little curation, uneven quality |
| Inkitt | Data-driven selection | Low interactivity |
| Kindle Vella | Episodic format | Minimal feedback loop |
| Millie | Strong curation | Weak creator ecosystem |
| Brunch | Great writing tools | Hard for emerging authors to be seen |
| Naver Series | Strong genre content | Low engagement |

None of them combines author growth with active reader participation. That became the positioning: Bookiverse bridges the author–reader gap with an ecosystem centred on growth, participation and relationships.

**Personas.** I turned the interviews into two personas with empathy maps. Jenny, 27, writes consistently but rarely reaches readers: "As long as I'm sure my writing is being read, I can write it until the end." For her, Bookiverse offers a feed that gives emerging writers fair exposure and a view of how readers respond. David, 31, values finding hidden gems: "Rather than a completed book, I want to share the process of the author's growth itself." For him, it offers a feed built for exploring new authors and a way to support them directly.

## Ideate

**The concept.** Bookiverse turns reading into participation and writing into growth. It rests on four parts: an interactive story feed for discovering emerging authors, a review and comment layer, a reader support system with flexible contributions, and a growth dashboard for writers.

**Two journeys that meet at support.** The reader moves from discover to read, support and reward. The author moves from publish to reach, get support and grow. Support is the point where the two journeys meet: it turns reading into participation and gives writing a way to continue.

![The reader journey and the author journey, connected at the support step](images/bookiverse/process.svg)

The main decisions, from low-fidelity wireframes to the final screens:

- **New authors first.** The home screen has the reader's own shelf and, below it, recommended books by new authors, each with a short description before the first page.
- **Read before you decide.** The first chapter is free. At its end the app asks "Do you like this story?" and invites the reader to become a member of the book's community.
- **Show what support brings.** Before anyone pays, the support screen lists what a supporter gets: all chapters, behind-the-scenes episodes and character diaries, the author's commentary and a story-inspired goods kit.
- **Feedback in words, not only likes.** After supporting, the reader is asked for stars and a written review, in the author's voice: "I want to hear your voice!" This follows the author interview, where two real comments counted for more than hundreds of likes.
- **Every ask can be skipped.** Each prompt to join, support or review comes with "No, thanks" or "Maybe later".
- **Rewards in one place.** A shelf shows the books a reader supports, with their rewards, such as a limited-edition planner and tote bag for supporters.
- **A dashboard for the author.** Three numbers at the top, members, reviews and support received, each with a graph and a list of the latest activity, so the author can see that the book is being read.

## Feedback and next steps

**Expected impact**

- **Readers:** from passive consumers to creative partners who take part through feedback and rewards
- **Authors:** fairer exposure, and a move from feeling unread to growing with their readers
- **The ecosystem:** direct financial support and a lasting bond between authors and readers

**Feedback at the mid-term presentation**

- The professor called the project's structure and research path "very nice, very logical"
- The slides looked close to a wireframe, with generic typography
- Once the app's own typeface and colours were settled, the presentation should carry them too

**Feedback at the final presentation**

- The professor responded well to the logical flow of the project and to the idea of a platform built on interaction between readers and authors
- As a next step, he suggested developing the business model further, for example with a subscription alongside reader support

**Next steps**

- Bookiverse has not been tested with users yet. I want to choose at least one real author and one real reader, let them use the prototype and collect their feedback
- Work out the revenue model: reader support, a subscription, or both
